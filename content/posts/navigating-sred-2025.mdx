The SR&ED program has long been one of the most powerful tools available to Canadian companies investing in research and development. But 2025 is shaping up to be a year of transition - one that rewards well-prepared claimants and creates headaches for those who treat the program as a routine filing.

## What Is Changing

The Canada Revenue Agency has been steadily increasing its review activity for SR&ED claims. Audit rates have climbed meaningfully over the past three years. CRA is applying more rigorous scrutiny to technical narratives, placing greater emphasis on contemporaneous documentation, and examining contractor costs with increasing care.

This doesn't mean the program is becoming inaccessible. It means the bar for a strong claim is higher than it was five years ago - and companies that rely on thin narratives or reconstruct documentation after the fact are facing more risk than ever before.

## What CRA Is Looking For

At the core of every SR&ED claim is a three-part test:

**1. Technological uncertainty.** Your work must have begun from a point of genuine uncertainty - a problem that couldn't be resolved by a competent practitioner applying standard industry techniques.

**2. Systematic investigation.** The work must have followed a structured, hypothesis-driven process. Exploratory tinkering doesn't qualify; systematic experimentation does.

**3. Technological advancement.** The work must have produced new knowledge - even a negative result (i.e., "this approach doesn't work") can qualify as an advancement if it was genuinely unknown.

CRA reviewers are trained to look for whether these elements are genuinely present in a claim, and increasingly, they are looking for evidence - not just assertions.

## The Documentation Imperative

One of the most consistent themes in CRA audit findings is inadequate contemporaneous documentation. "Contemporaneous" means records created at or near the time the work was being done - not reconstructed months later when the claim is being prepared.

For software companies, this typically means time tracking logs broken down by project and activity, Git commit histories and sprint records, architectural decision documents, and records of what was tested, what failed, and why.

For hardware and biotech companies, lab notebooks, test result records, iteration logs, and equipment usage records are the core documentation types.

The practical implication: documentation habits need to be built into your engineering and R&D workflows year-round - not bolted on at claim preparation time.

## Contractor Costs Under the Microscope

CRA applies specific rules to SR&ED contractor costs that many companies get wrong. For arm's-length contractors, only 65% of total invoiced amounts are includable in SR&ED claims - and only for work actually performed in Canada.

Companies that have historically included 100% of contractor costs, or that have used offshore contractors without careful tracking of which work was done in Canada, are facing increased exposure in the current audit environment.

## What This Means for Your 2025 Claim

If you haven't already, 2025 is the year to:

- Review and strengthen your documentation practices before fiscal year-end
- Conduct a mid-year technical scoping call with your SR&ED consultant to identify qualifying activities early
- Audit your contractor arrangements for SR&ED eligibility and inclusion rate compliance
- Ensure your technical narratives are written to CRA's three-part test — not just as project descriptions

## The Opportunity Remains Significant

None of this should suggest that SR&ED is no longer worth pursuing. It remains one of the most valuable non-dilutive funding sources available to Canadian companies. A well-prepared CCPC can still recover 35% of qualifying expenditures in fully refundable federal investment tax credits, with provincial credits stacking on top.

The difference is that preparation quality now matters more than it did before. Companies with strong documentation, rigorous technical narratives, and experienced advisors are claiming what they're owed with confidence. Companies that cut corners are leaving money on the table — or worse, having claims reduced or denied after CRA review.
